Five problems I could not stop noticing

and what I built for each

I am Jessie Chen, 陈亦奇, an AI product manager candidate finishing an MA in Computational Social Science at the University of Chicago, class of 2027. In the past year I shipped five AI products on my own; three of them run in production every day. This page tells them in the order I met the problems, not the order I built the products.

The products and their case pages are in Chinese; this page is in English. Numbers were read in September 2026 and each one names where it came from.

One

In class, the question dies before you finish typing it.

Online lectures. Noticed in my own notes.

So I built Lecture Companion.

The bottleneck is input friction, not model capability. A question became one click on a transcript sentence, and multimodal retrieval fills in the whiteboard and the formulas. The North Star is after-class review, not activity during class. Across nine sessions there were 63 marks, 59 of them with zero typing (product dashboard). Multimodal retrieval shipped in three stages with no rollbacks (release log), and the ASR cost came down to a quarter (usage ledger).

Two

You save everything and never go back.

Xiaohongshu bookmarks. Mine, and everyone's.

So I built xhs-threads.

Organizing never happens because it needs the user to start. Here the user only saves. The AI threads the bookmarks into trains of thought, writes the notes, and an independent reviewer model checks the output before anything is published. The archive holds 11 threads and 63 nodes (production database); 90 percent of calls run on the light model tier (call ledger), at ¥0.05 to ¥0.10 per item.

Three

You say yes to things you will not do.

Group chats. Every week.

So I built Nous.

Other bots learn how you talk; Nous learns how you decide. A nine-dimension cognitive model judges whether to reply, drafts in your way, and raises a warning when you are about to agree to something you will not follow through on. It runs as a Feishu bot, a Mac floating assistant and a web app, all three in daily use. Behavior prediction went from 49 to 71 percent, and the correction loop is closed: your corrections feed the model.

Four

The job search is blocked by people, not information.

My own search, and my friends'.

So I built JobPilot.

Existing tools stop at the behavior layer. JobPilot plugs in a cognitive model and language samples to give a personal diagnosis and messaging that sounds like you. It is open source: 349 jobs collected and 37 scored high (collection database), 31 CLI commands and 544 tests with CI green (repository). All six product trade-offs have a written why.

Five

The data is public and nobody reads it.

Shanghai musical-theatre ticketing. I was already in the audience.

So I built MusiClaw.

Public ticketing data turned into a product that updates itself every day: seat-map snapshots for 86 shows, cast attribution analysis, and four content pipelines feeding a real data account, with a fixed 24-hour review after every post. What the AI may and may not decide is written down, not assumed.

How I pick a problem

  1. Pain point before solution. Find the layer the bottleneck lives in before building; get the layer wrong and no feature fixes it.
  2. Staged rollout and attribution. One layer of change per stage, so a problem points at its own layer.
  3. Define the metric before the number. Marks reviewed after class, not made in class. Every number names its dashboard and read date.
  4. Cost is a product parameter. Every call logged and routed by tier; a shadow run proves quality holds before cost is cut.
